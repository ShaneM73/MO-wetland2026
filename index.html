Choosing the store size (station rain gauge)

Timing error: median days, model minus observed, for the autumn date each series first rises above its own median and the spring/summer date it first falls below it. Negative = model early. The first version of the model used a 100 mm store and wet up 42 days and dried out 25 days too early; every map and chart on this page now uses 250 mm.

The 50 cm and 100 cm probes step up in mid-December 2021: wet-season readings at 50 cm jump from about 0.30 to 0.45 m³/m³ and stay there, in a year that was not unusually wet. This looks like a sensor change, and it is why winters from 2022 on reach higher in the observed line. Using only 2010–2021 gives the same store size (250 mm: wet-up −1 day, dry-out −2 days, r 0.84).

Finding NWI wetlands within like land coverAUC

AUC here compares NWI wetland cells with non-wetland cells of the same NLCD 2021 land cover. Forest = deciduous, evergreen, mixed forest and woody wetland, scored with NWI forested/shrub wetlands. Open = shrub, grassland, pasture, crops and herbaceous wetland, scored with NWI emergent wetlands. Comparing across the whole watershed instead mostly measures forest against open land. 0.5 = no skill; for radar, above 0.5 means wetlands are brighter (or brighten more after rain).

Sentinel-1 C-band radar

OPERA RTC-S1 (30 m, VV and VH), one ascending burst at about 7 pm local, 2026. Passes are grouped by the same rule as NISAR: wet = at least 40 mm of rain in the 7 days before the pass (hourly Stage IV up to the pass) and a soil store of at least 190 mm; dry = at most 15 mm and at most 180 mm. Winter = leaves off, January to early April; a pass on a freezing day (23 Jan) is dropped.

AUC: chance that a wetland cell gets a higher probability than a non-wetland cell. AP (average precision) summarises how clean the top of the ranking is; with wetlands only of cells, a random map scores AP ≈ . Cut-offs are chosen on the held-out predictions. The ROC cut-off (Youden: largest recall minus false-alarm rate) treats the 2.5% wetland class and the 97.5% dry class equally, so it accepts many false alarms; the F1 cut-off balances precision and recall on the wetland class itself and maps about NWI's area. The model learns to reproduce NWI, errors included: blue outside NWI is a candidate for checking, not proof of a missed wetland.

What to check

  • Fen site. Median 1 m TWI , wetter than of the watershed. The wettest quarter of the site (TWI above ) is above the watershed's 95th percentile (). On the 16 m grid the fen ranks at the 94th percentile.
  • 1 m vs 16 m TWI. Switch between the two. At 1 m the floodplain breaks into thin threads of high TWI with drier ground between them. The NWI wetlands sit on that drier-looking ground, so 1 m TWI scores below chance (AUC 0.38). At 16 m the whole valley floor reads wet and AUC rises to 0.89. Averaging the 1 m TWI up to 16 m does not fix it; the DEM itself has to be coarser.
  • Hydroperiod. Mean days per year the model holds a cell at saturation (2003–2025, Stage IV rainfall, gridMET evapotranspiration, 250 mm root-zone store). With SSURGO soil the floodplain gravels drain fast and many NWI wetlands look dry. With uniform soil the map tracks 16 m TWI more closely. Open water (NHD) is masked and shows as grey relief.
  • Saturation calendar. The model never dries the whole fen: 3 of its 19 cells stay saturated every day. The rest is wettest from December to June and drains from July to November, most in dry years such as 2012, 2014 and 2017. Saturation peaks in May, about two months after the measured soil water at Salem, because water from the hillslopes reaches the valley floor late. NWI codes the fen B (saturated most of the year), which suggests groundwater keeps it wet when rain alone would not. Testing that needs water-table records at the fen.
  • NISAR radar. L-band backscatter is almost unrelated to TWI (rank correlation about 0) and much weaker on its own (AUC about 0.6 against 0.8–0.9 for TWI). Wetlands in forest are brighter in HH, as expected from trunk–water double bounce. NWI emergent wetlands brighten more after rain than other open ground, but there are only 327 such cells. Switch to NISAR HH wet − dry and look along the valley floors.
  • Combined model. Terrain alone (TWI, slope, hydroperiod, land cover) already reaches AUC 0.97 against NWI; most of the gain over TWI comes from slope. Adding radar barely moves AUC but makes the top of the ranking cleaner (AP 0.46 → 0.53) and helps most in open land (AUC 0.74 → 0.82 with NISAR). Single radar features look unimportant in the shuffle test only because the radar features carry overlapping information; dropping all radar is what lowers the score. Barton Fen's probability falls from (terrain) to (with radar): its radar signature, bright but unresponsive to rain, is unlike the mostly forested NWI wetlands the model learns from.
  • Groundwater proxies. Height above the nearest stream is the strongest single predictor of NWI found so far (AUC 0.97 in forest, 0.91 in open land, against 0.91 and 0.80 for TWI), and adding it lifts the model to AP 0.70. The SSURGO water table and drainage class run the wrong way: the soil survey rates the floodplain gravels as deep and well drained, and it rates the fen excessively drained with no water table within 2 m. The fen’s median probability falls as groundwater inputs are added ( in the best model), but it still ranks near the 95th percentile of all cells and is wet at the lenient cut-off; most of the drop comes from SSURGO, which wrongly rates the fen well drained with no hydric soil. NWI here is mostly floodplain forest, and the model ranks emergent wetlands by valley position rather than as a type of their own (see the hold-out table below). Finding fens will need their own labels (field points) rather than more NWI-trained inputs.
  • Landsat thermal and seeps. Switch to Winter-warm, summer-cool or Snow-free and look at the fen: it is one of the few valley-floor spots that stays warm in winter, cool in summer and clear of snow, as groundwater at ~13 °C would make it. NWI wetlands as a whole show no such signal, so adding thermal and geology to the NWI-trained model does not help it (AP against ). Seep candidates applies the fen's signature to the whole valley floor without NWI: these are the places to visit next, after ruling out roads.
  • ECOSTRESS nights and Sentinel-2 phenology. Switch to Warm winter nights: ECOSTRESS night passes have no sunshine in them, and the fen does not stand out on winter nights, while the daytime ECOSTRESS passes repeat the Landsat pattern. That argues the Landsat signature is mostly sun and surface cover, not groundwater heat. Late-summer moisture from Sentinel-2 at 10 m shows the fen as a seasonal meadow, greener than its surroundings in June but no moister in September and brown in winter. Sentinel-2 phenology does give the NWI model a small lift in open land (AP 0.70 → 0.72, open-land AUC 0.88 → 0.91), mostly from autumn moisture, so it is now part of the map model; ECOSTRESS does not. A seep too small or too weak to warm a 70 m pixel on a winter night is still possible; a drone thermal flight on a cold clear morning is the way to settle it.
